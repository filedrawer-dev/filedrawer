The Last Keeper of Skerry Rock

The supply boat stopped coming in October, and by November Maren had stopped expecting it.

She kept the lamp anyway. Every evening she climbed the ninety-one steps with the brass can of oil, trimmed the wick, wound the clockwork, and watched the beam begin its slow walk across the water. There were no ships. There had been no ships since the summer. But a lamp that is not lit is only a tower, her father used to say, and she had never found the argument against it.

On the ninth night of the month there was a knock at the door.

This was not possible. The rock was forty paces across and she had walked every one of them that afternoon, looking for driftwood. She stood on the stairs with the oil can in her hand and listened to the knock come again, three times, patient.

"Who is it?" she said, feeling foolish.

"The relief," said a voice. It was a tired voice, a little hoarse, with an accent from further down the coast than she had ever been.

She opened the door. A man stood there in an oilskin, perfectly dry, though the spray was coming over the rock in sheets. He had a canvas bag over one shoulder and a lantern that gave no light.

"You've kept it well," he said, looking up at the beam. "They said you would."

"Who said?"

He seemed not to hear. He stepped past her, set his bag by the stove as if he had done it a thousand times, and began to climb the stairs. At the first turn he looked back.

"There's a boat at the landing," he said. "It won't wait long."

Maren went to the door. There was a boat at the landing, small and grey, riding the swell as easily as a gull. Nobody was in it.

Above her, the lamp turned. She put down the oil can, and after a while she took her coat from the hook.
