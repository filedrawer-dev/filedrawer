Growing tomatoes outdoors in a cool climate: what has worked for me

Choose the right variety. Forget the big beefsteaks unless you have a greenhouse. Small-fruited and early varieties ripen before the weather turns. Bush types need no pinching out and do well in pots; cordon types give more fruit per square foot but want a stake and weekly attention.

Sow late, not early. Mid March to early April on a warm windowsill is plenty. Seedlings sown in January get leggy and sulk. Pot on when the first true leaves show, burying the stem up to the seed leaves each time; it roots along the buried part.

Harden off properly. Ten days of going out by day and in at night before planting. Plant out only when the nights stay above 10 degrees, which here means the end of May.

Plant deep, in the sunniest spot you have, against a south wall if you can. Work in plenty of compost. Space cordons 45cm apart.

Water evenly. A good soak twice a week beats a sprinkle every day. Irregular watering gives you split fruit and blossom end rot, the black leathery patch on the bottom. Water the soil, never the leaves.

Feed once the first truss has set: a high-potash feed every week or ten days.

Pinch out the side shoots on cordons, the little shoots that grow in the joint between leaf and stem. Stop the plant, cut off the growing tip, after four or five trusses outdoors. It won't ripen more than that.

Blight. In a wet August you will get it: brown blotches on leaves, then stems, then the fruit rots. Take off affected leaves at once, keep air moving through the plants, and if it takes hold pick everything green and ripen it indoors in a drawer with a banana.

At the end of the season green tomatoes make very good chutney.
